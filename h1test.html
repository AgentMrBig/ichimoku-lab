The H1 Finding

The Ichimoku engine sets its bias on the daily chart and times entries on a lower one. It has always timed on the H4. This study asks a simple question — is H4 the right entry timeframe? — and the answer turns out to be different for each pair. All figures are R-space on 2 years of 1-minute data (2023–2025), spread charged.

✅
EUR/USD
H4 → H1
4× the net R, survives 3× spread. The clear winner.
✅
USD/JPY
H4 → H1
2.4× the net R, holds up under 3× spread.
🛡️
XAU/USD
stays H4
H1 looks great until you charge real slippage — gold's wide spread breaks it. H4 stays home.

1 · Timeframe sweep in-sample, both directions

Net R and profit factor at each entry timeframe. Lower TF = more trades. For the majors, more trades meant far more R at the same quality; gold's quality (PF) fell as it sped up.

2 · Out-of-sample split train ½ → test ½ (unseen)

The overfit test. Train on the first year, measure on the second the model never saw. H1 doesn't just survive — every pair's test half beats its train half. That's the fingerprint of a real edge, not a curve fit.

For contrast, H4 gold's famous PF 2.88 was a first-half mirage: train PF 4.41 → test PF 1.84. It front-loaded.

3 · Spread stress the live-slippage tax

The one that decides shippability. Re-charge each H1 trade at 2× and 3× the assumed spread to simulate real broker slippage. The majors shrug it off. Gold — short holds, wide spread — collapses.

The takeaway. Entry timeframe isn't one knob for the whole engine — it's per-pair. The two majors were being throttled on H4 and belong on H1, where the edge is 2–4× larger and survives triple slippage. Gold stays on H4: on H1 it looks even better on paper, but that paper edge evaporates the moment you charge realistic spread, because gold's spread is wide and its H1 trades are too short to pay for it. Same logic, three pairs, two different homes. Not yet live — this is the case for the change, pending a demo run to confirm live fills match the model.
